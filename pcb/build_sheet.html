Tank Pump Controller PCB v1.0

Build sheet · 100 × 98 mm, 2 layers · plugs into the wiring of the main guide (Figure 6) · ESP32 DevKit V1 30-pin

The board carries everything that was loose wiring in the main guide: fuse F1, a surge absorber, the HLK-5M05, the relay that switches the contactor coil, the ESP32 sockets and the sensor terminal. You only connect wires to its screw terminals. Pump current never goes through the board: the supply cable, the contactor main contacts and the pump cable stay exactly as in the guide.

Isolation. 230 V copper is kept at least 6.0 mm from all low-voltage copper on both layers (checked after the ground fill), with a milled slot under the relay between its contacts and its coil. The HLK-5M05 and the relay are the only parts that bridge the gap, and both are rated for it.

4. Ordering the board

Option A: assembled by the factory (all parts already soldered)

The factory buys and solders every part in section 3. You receive finished boards and only plug in the ESP32, push in the fuse and connect the wires.

  1. On jlcpcb.com, upload pcb\out\tank_controller_gerbers.zip and choose the same board settings as in Option B, quantity 5.
  2. Switch on PCB Assembly: Economic, top side, assembled quantity 2 or 5 (2 is cheaper, and the other 3 boards come bare as spares). Leave tooling holes at “Added by JLCPCB”.
  3. Upload pcb\out\assembly\tank_controller_bom.csv as the BOM and pcb\out\assembly\tank_controller_cpl.csv as the CPL (placement) file. All 17 lines should match a part by its LCSC number. If one is out of stock, pick the suggested equivalent with the same package and value, and for J5 and the relay K1 accept only the same footprint.
  4. In the placement preview, check that the polarised parts sit the way the silkscreen shows: the LED flat sides, D1's stripe at K, C1's − stripe away from + and Q1's flat face. Rotate any that are wrong in the preview. (The HLK, relay and fuse holder only fit one way.)
  5. Order. The quote shows the exact price; expect roughly $110–150 before shipping and customs for 5 boards with 2 assembled (most of it is fixed per-order fees, so assembling all 5 costs only about $20 more). Delivered to India this is about ₹12,000–18,000. Indian assemblers (Lion Circuits, PCB Power, Circuitwala) accept the same three files for a quote, with no customs.
Not fitted by the factory: the 1 A slow-blow 5×20 mm fuse (push it into F1 yourself), the ESP32 and the M3 standoffs. Before the first power-up, still do the stage 0 multimeter test in section 6.

Option B: bare board, you solder the parts

  1. Upload pcb\out\tank_controller_gerbers.zip to a PCB maker. In India: Lion Circuits, PCB Power or Robu PCB (about ₹700–1,500 for 5 boards, 5–8 days). Abroad: JLCPCB or PCBWay (about $2 for the boards plus $15–25 shipping and customs, 10–15 days).
  2. Settings: 2 layers, 100 × 98 mm (the site reads this from the zip), FR-4, 1.6 mm thick, 1 oz copper, HASL lead-free finish, any solder mask colour, quantity 5. Leave everything else at the default.
  3. If the site asks about the small rectangular cut-out near the relay: it is an intentional milled slot (isolation between the relay coil and its contacts) and must be kept.

5. Soldering order (bare board only)

  1. Flat parts first: resistors R1–R5 (R1, R4, R5 are 1 k; R2 is 10 k; R3 is 330 Ω), then D1 with its black stripe at the end marked K.
  2. LED1, LED2: short leg (flat side) into the square pad. Q1: flat face matches the silkscreen outline. C2, then C1 with its stripe (−) away from the + mark.
  3. Female headers J6 and J7. Plug the ESP32 into them while soldering, so they end up straight and at the right spacing; then take it out again.
  4. Screw terminals J1–J5 with the wire openings facing the board edge, then the fuse holder, RV1, K1 and finally the HLK-5M05. Use plenty of solder on the 230 V joints and terminals.
  5. Inspect the 230 V area with a magnifier: no solder bridges, no flux balls or wire strands between pads. Clean off flux with isopropyl alcohol.
  6. Firmware: in the sketch, keep #define CONTROLLER_PCB 1. The relay then runs on GPIO32, active HIGH (with 0, the sketch goes back to the relay module on GPIO23, active LOW).

6. Testing, in three stages

StageDo thisIt is right when
0. Multimeter
board unpowered, fuse fitted		Measure resistance from J1 L to J1 N; from each of J1–J4 to sensor GND; and across the fuse holder clips.L to N: no short (high or slowly rising reading, because the HLK input capacitor charges). Any 230 V terminal to sensor GND: open (over limit). Fuse: about 0 Ω.
1. Bench, USB only
no 230 V anywhere		Plug the ESP32 into the sockets, connect USB to a laptop, flash the sketch. Wire the sensor to J5 through a short CAT6 piece and point it at the floor. Switch the pump ON and OFF from the app.Distance readings are steady in the serial monitor and the app. The RELAY LED lights and K1 clicks with each ON / OFF (most DevKit V1 boards feed USB 5 V to VIN; if yours doesn't, check this in stage 2 instead).
2. In the box, 230 V, no pump
pump wires 9–11 not connected		Mount the board, connect all board terminals (section 1) and wires 1–3, 7–8. Unplug the USB cable, close the box, switch the MCB on.
S1 on AUTO: switch ON and OFF from the app. S1 on MAN. S1 on 0.		PWR LED on, ESP32 connects. AUTO: app ON makes K1 click, KM pull in and the lamp light; app OFF releases all three. MAN: KM and lamp on whatever the app says. 0: everything off.
3. With the pumpMCB off. Connect pump wires 9–11. MCB on, then run the full test sequence in section 10 of the main guide.The pump starts and stops with KM, stops at 95 %, and the fault tests in the guide pass.
Safety. The top part of the board carries 230 V whenever the MCB is on, including the HLK pins, the fuse holder and the relay contact pins underneath. Only work on it with the MCB off, and never connect USB to the ESP32 while the board is powered from 230 V. Have an electrician check the box before the first full power-up.
